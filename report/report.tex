\section{Problem and dataset}
%======================================================================

Closed-set face identification is the task of identifying the person in an
image from a fixed set of enrolled individuals. Posed directly in pixel space the problem is badly conditioned:
a $64\times64$ greyscale image is a point in $\mathbb{R}^{4096}$ and the dataset
used here contains 400 such vectors, so features outnumber observations by about
ten to one. The sample covariance matrix is singular by construction and no
per-class density is estimable.

Face images do not fill $\mathbb{R}^{4096}$. They concentrate near a much
lower-dimensional structure, because faces have a common geometry. The
Eigenfaces method \citep{turk1991} is built on this. The linear subspace
holding most of the variation across a set of faces is estimated, and
classification takes place inside it. That pipeline is implemented here from
first principles, including the principal component analysis itself. The
central question of the project is:

\begin{quote}
How much dimensionality can be removed from facial images before
identity-discriminating information is significantly lost?
\end{quote}

Two criteria give different answers. Under \emph{reconstruction}, what counts
is how much pixel variance is retained; under \emph{discrimination}, how well
identities can still be told apart. The main finding here is that the two
criteria differ by a factor of about four, for a reason visible in the
component structure.

The Olivetti (ORL) database \citep{samaria1994} consists of 400 greyscale images
of 40 subjects, ten each, normalised to $[0,1]$. Within a subject the images
vary in lighting, expression, and the presence of glasses. All are cropped,
frontal, and roughly aligned against a dark background, so the experiments
isolate the recognition method from any face \emph{detection} front end. The
redundancy that motivates the method is measurable. Among 300 sampled pixels
the mean absolute pairwise correlation is $0.26$ and $10.4\%$ of pairs exceed
$|r|=0.5$.

%======================================================================
\section{Eigenface method}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Principal components}

Each image is flattened row-major into $x_i\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$, $d=4096$, and
stacked into $X=[x_1,\dots,x_N]^{T}$ with $N=400$. Writing
$\mu=\frac{1}{N}\sum_i x_i$ and $A=X-\mathbf{1}\mu^{T}$, the sample covariance is
\begin{equation}
\Sigma=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_i-\mu)(x_i-\mu)^{T}=\frac{1}{N-1}A^{T}A.
\label{eq:cov}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth]{04_mean_face.png}
\caption{The mean face $\mu$ over all 400 images, and three mean-centred images
$x_i-\mu$ (red above the mean, blue below).}
\label{fig:mean}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:mean} shows $\mu$ and three of the mean-centred images. PCA
maximises the projected variance $v^{T}\Sigma v$ subject to $v^{T}v=1$. With
a Lagrange multiplier, $\mathcal{L}=v^{T}\Sigma v-\lambda(v^{T}v-1)$, and
$\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial v=0$ yields the eigenvalue problem
\begin{equation}
\Sigma v_i=\lambda_i v_i .
\label{eq:eig}
\end{equation}

By Equation~\eqref{eq:eig}, the stationary points of the projected variance
are the eigenvectors of the covariance $\Sigma$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:cov},
and since $v_i^{T}\Sigma v_i=\lambda_i$, each eigenvalue is the variance along
the corresponding eigenvector. Keeping the top $k$
eigenvectors as columns of $W_k$ gives the rank-$k$ subspace of least expected
squared reconstruction error \citep{eckart1936}. $\Sigma$ is real symmetric, so
$W_k^{T}W_k=I_k$.

\subsection{Snapshot trick}

$\Sigma$ is $4096\times4096$ and singular: it sums $N_{\text{train}}$ rank-one
terms under one linear constraint, so
$\operatorname{rank}(\Sigma)\le N_{\text{train}}-1=279$ and at most 279 of its
4096 eigenvalues are non-zero. Take instead the Gram matrix
$G=\frac{1}{N-1}AA^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}$. If $Gu_i=\lambda_i u_i$,
left-multiplying by $A^{T}$ gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{N-1}A^{T}AA^{T}u_i=\lambda_i A^{T}u_i
\iff \Sigma\,(A^{T}u_i)=\lambda_i\,(A^{T}u_i),
\label{eq:snapshot}
\end{equation}
so $A^{T}u_i$ is an eigenvector of $\Sigma$ with the same eigenvalue. By
Equation~\eqref{eq:snapshot}, the non-zero spectrum of a $4096\times4096$
matrix follows from a $280\times280$ eigenproblem. On the machine used here,
fitting took $0.021$~s by this route against $12.7$~s for the direct
decomposition, a 614-fold reduction. The resulting vectors are normalised to
unit length.

Each $v_i$ lies in $\mathbb{R}^{4096}$, the space of the images themselves, so
it reshapes to $64\times64$ and displays as a \emph{pattern of deviation from
the mean face}. As a linear combination of training faces,
$v_i\propto\sum_j u_{ij}(x_j-\mu)$, it is itself face-like, hence the name, and a
face is then represented by $k$ coefficients over that shared
basis: $x\approx\mu+\sum_{j\le k} z_jv_j$ with $z=W_k^{T}(x-\mu)$.

\subsection{Classification and verification}

A test face is projected to $z=W_k^{T}(x-\mu)$ and classified by $k$-nearest
neighbours \citep{duda2001}. The non-parametric rule follows from the sample
size. With 40 classes and 7 training images each, a full Gaussian per class
would have $k+k(k+1)/2$ parameters to estimate from 7 observations. Because $W_k$ is
orthonormal, distances in the projected space equal distances between the
pixel-space reconstructions. Two variants recur: \emph{plain} projection,
where components keep their natural scale so high-variance directions dominate,
and \emph{whitened} projection $\tilde z_j=z_j/\sqrt{\lambda_j}$, which
rescales every component to unit variance and amounts to a Mahalanobis distance
inside the retained subspace.

Checked against the exact SVD solver in scikit-learn, the implementation gives a
maximum relative eigenvalue error of $5.0\times10^{-15}$, a minimum absolute
eigenvector dot product of $1.0000000000$, and a maximum reconstruction
difference of $1.6\times10^{-14}$.

%======================================================================
\section{Experimental design}
%======================================================================

Each subject contributes $n_{\text{train}}$ images to training and the rest to
test, since a uniformly random split would leave some subjects with no training
images and so unidentifiable by construction. The default is 7 train / 3 test
per subject: $N_{\text{train}}=280$, $N_{\text{test}}=120$.

$\mu$ and $W$ come from training images only, and test images are projected
using those fixed parameters, because fitting PCA on the full 400 images before
splitting leaks information into the basis and inflates the accuracy reported
from it. With 120 test images a single split has a standard error near
$0.024$, so every figure reported here is the mean over 10 independent splits and
is given with its standard deviation.

\section{Recognition results}
%======================================================================

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.94\textwidth]{06_eigenfaces.png}
\caption{The first 16 Eigenfaces, with the share of total variance for each component.}
\label{fig:eigenfaces}
\end{figure}

The first three components in Figure~\ref{fig:eigenfaces} are low in spatial
frequency and, on inspection, correspond to whole-image photometric structure
rather than facial detail. $v_1$ is a broad contrast pattern separating the brow and
eye band from the rest of the face, $v_2$ is nearly flat and encodes overall
luminance, and $v_3$ is a top-to-bottom intensity gradient. None of the three
distinguishes one person from another. Recognisable structure begins at $v_4$:
spectacle frames, hairline, moustaches, and changes around the mouth. Later
components become more localised and higher in frequency, with identity
information alongside sampling noise specific to these training images.

Meeting the variance thresholds set in the brief takes 59, 103, and 198
components for 90\%, 95\%, and 99\% respectively, that is 1.44\%, 2.51\%, and
4.83\% of the original 4096 dimensions. The share of total variance is 23.4\%
for the first component alone and 66.1\% for the first ten.

In Figure~\ref{fig:acc} and Table~\ref{tab:acc} the two curves differ in
shape, and the contrast between them is the clearest result here.

\centering

\caption{Recognition accuracy against $k$, plain and whitened projections,
mean $\pm$ s.d.\ over 10 splits.}
\label{fig:acc}
\end{figure}

\begin{table}[htbp]

\caption{Recognition accuracy against the number of retained components.}
\label{tab:acc}
\begin{tabular}{rll@{\hskip 2.2em}rll}
\toprule
$k$ & Plain & Whitened & $k$ & Plain & Whitened\\
\midrule

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The plain curve rises and then flattens without falling, which follows from the
geometry of the projection. $W$ is orthonormal, so with every component
retained the Euclidean distance in Eigenface space equals the pixel distance
exactly, and adding components moves 1-NN monotonically towards the
full-dimensional answer. Trailing components have tiny eigenvalues and
contribute almost nothing to a Euclidean distance, so under this metric surplus
components cost only computation.

Under whitening the curve rises, peaks at $k=20$, then falls from $0.944$ to $0.778$
at $k=200$. Whitening divides component $j$ by $\sqrt{\lambda_j}$, which for a
trailing component means dividing by a very small number and so scaling it up to the
same variance as the leading ones. The $i$-th eigenvalue, however, comes from
280 observations in 4096 dimensions. For large $i$ that estimate is
increasingly dominated by sampling noise, and the direction then reflects
quirks of the particular training images more than a property of the
population. Whitening amplifies exactly that noise, producing the familiar
overfitting signature.

The useful number of components is set by how many directions are estimated
reliably from the sample, not by how many exist mathematically, and whether the
unreliable ones degrade accuracy depends on whether the metric gives them
weight. Both curves follow from this. It is why they diverge, and why ``how
many components should I keep?'' has no answer from the representation
alone.

At $k=75$, 1-NN with Euclidean distance gives accuracy $0.9367\pm0.0137$, macro
precision $0.9507$, macro recall $0.9367$, and macro $F_1$ $0.9339$.
Macro-averaging weights every subject equally, so failure on a few individuals
is not masked by a good average. On the reference split 8 of 120 test images
are misclassified and the confusion matrix is close to diagonal, with no pair
of subjects mutually confused.

Across distances at $k=75$ and 1-NN, Manhattan distance gives $0.9442$,
Euclidean $0.9367$, and cosine $0.9300$, a margin well under one standard
deviation. The effect of neighbourhood size is larger than that of the distance
function.
Euclidean accuracy falls to $0.854$ at 3-NN and $0.805$ at 5-NN. With 7
training images per subject a 5-NN vote often includes images of other
identities from neighbouring clusters, so the usual argument that larger $n$
smooths noise rests on a sample density absent here.

\subsection{Reconstruction fidelity}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth]{12_reconstructions.png}
\caption{Reconstructions of three test faces at increasing $k$.}
\label{fig:recon}
\end{figure}

Reconstruction maps a projection back by $\hat x=\mu+W_kW_k^{T}(x-\mu)$, and
since $W_kW_k^{T}$ is an orthogonal projector, the residual lies wholly in the
discarded directions, giving
$\mathbb{E}\|x-\hat x\|^2=\sum_{j>k}\lambda_j$. In Figure~\ref{fig:recon}, at
$k=1$ each face is the mean face with a photometric adjustment. Identity
becomes visually recognisable around $k=20$ to $30$, and past $k\approx100$ the
changes are confined to fine texture. Test RMSE falls from $0.083$ at $k=10$ to
$0.050$ at $k=100$ and $0.041$ at $k=279$, with PSNR $21.8$, $26.2$, and
$28.1$~dB.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{13_recon_vs_recognition.png}
\caption{Reconstruction error and recognition accuracy against $k$.}
\label{fig:diverge}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:diverge} shows reconstruction error still falling well past the
point where recognition stops improving, because the variance removed across
that range, fine skin texture, sensor noise, small expression detail,
contributes to pixel fidelity and not to identity. Holding 95\% of variance
costs about four times the components required for recognition, $k=103$ against
$k=25$, and moves accuracy from $0.926$ to $0.935$ (measured at $k=100$), a
difference inside the split-to-split noise. A variance threshold is therefore
the wrong criterion for choosing $k$ in this recogniser: it is a measure of how
well the subspace reproduces images, whereas recognition depends on how well
the subspace separates identities.

\section{Critical analysis}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Enrolment size}

Enrolment set size is the strongest single influence measured. Accuracy runs
$0.547$, $0.698$, $0.774$, $0.820$, $0.867$, $0.923$, and $0.950$ for 1, 2, 3,
4, 5, 7, and 9 training images per subject, and has not saturated at nine. Two
mechanisms compound. Few reference images are enrolled per subject, so a test image
with an unseen expression has no near neighbour of the correct identity; and
the basis itself degrades, since at $n_{\text{tr}}=1$ only 39 components exist,
estimated from 40 images. The fall from $0.950$ at nine images to $0.547$ at
one is the familiar one-sample-per-person difficulty.

\subsection{Illumination mismatch}

Table~\ref{tab:illum} and Figure~\ref{fig:illum} report accuracy when test
images are multiplied by a left-to-right linear intensity ramp of increasing
strength, modelling a directional source absent at enrolment, with training
images untouched.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Recognition accuracy under increasing illumination mismatch.}
\label{tab:illum}
\begin{tabular}{lcccccc}
\toprule
Ramp strength & 0.0 & 0.2 & 0.3 & 0.4 & 0.5 & 0.7\\
\midrule
Euclidean    & 0.937 & 0.878 & 0.758 & 0.488 & 0.305 & 0.142\\
Cosine       & 0.930 & 0.869 & 0.726 & 0.471 & 0.308 & 0.181\\
Drop first 3 & 0.932 & \textbf{0.938} & \textbf{0.936} & \textbf{0.920} & \textbf{0.890} & \textbf{0.783}\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.76\textwidth]{15_illumination.png}
\caption{Left: the ramp at strengths 0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.7. Right: accuracy under
increasing illumination mismatch.}
\label{fig:illum}
\end{figure}

Euclidean 1-NN falls from $0.937$ to $0.142$ across the ramp. Of the two
mitigations tested, only one works.

Cosine distance gives no benefit: at each of the six ramp strengths its
accuracy is within $0.04$ of the Euclidean value. The expectation that it would help rested on a uniform
brightness change, which shifts a projection mainly in magnitude. This ramp varies
spatially, brightening one side of the face and darkening the other, so it
rotates the projection without lengthening it, and discarding magnitude
discards nothing relevant. Invariance to global scaling is of little use against
lighting with spatial structure, which real lighting generally has.

With the first three components removed, accuracy under the strongest ramp
is $0.783$, against $0.142$ for Euclidean 1-NN with those components, so those
three directions contain nearly the whole perturbation. This is
experimental support for the reading of the component structure given earlier:
the leading eigenfaces encode photometric conditions rather than identity.
Discarding the three directions with the largest share of variance in the
entire dataset improves recognition under illumination change. Variance ranking
and discriminative value differ, and at the top of the spectrum they oppose
each other.

Under matched illumination, dropping up to three components moves accuracy by
less than one standard deviation either way, and dropping beyond about five
costs real accuracy. Removing three discards 46\% of total variance and leaves
recognition unchanged. Discarding leading components is therefore insurance
against mismatched conditions, not a free gain.

\section{Research question}
%======================================================================

1-NN on the raw 4096 pixels, with no reduction, gives $0.9342\pm0.0142$, the
reference against which Table~\ref{tab:rq} sets three tolerance levels.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Dimensionality that can be removed at three tolerance levels.}
\label{tab:rq}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
Criterion & $k$ & Accuracy & Dimensions removed & Variance retained\\
\midrule
Matches peak      & 75 & 0.937 & 98.17\% & 92.4\%\\
Within 1\% of peak & 30 & 0.929 & 99.27\% & 82.7\%\\
Within 5\% of peak & 15 & 0.919 & 99.63\% & 72.7\%\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

About 99\% of the original dimensions can be discarded with no measurable loss
of identity information. At $k=25$ the Eigenface representation scores
$0.9258\pm0.0160$ against the raw-pixel $0.9342\pm0.0142$, nominally lower by
well under the split-to-split noise, so the two are indistinguishable at this
sample size. The 164-fold compression costs nothing in accuracy and gains
nothing either.

Measured end to end, the Eigenface pipeline runs slower than raw 1-NN,
$16.4$~ms against $3.3$~ms per split, because it includes fitting PCA on every
split. The benefit of the reduction is at query time and at scale: each comparison
costs 25 multiplications instead of 4096 and the gallery is smaller by the same
factor, but across 400 images that saving does not cover the
one-off decomposition.

Three qualifications apply. The bound comes from sample size, not from faces;
the training set yields at most $N_{\text{train}}-1=279$ components, so
achievable compression depends on how many images were available rather than
on any intrinsic dimensionality of human faces. The threshold depends on the
criterion, since under reconstruction the answer is several times larger. It
depends on operating conditions too, because every figure here comes from
images captured much like the training images. A compression lossless under
matched conditions need not stay lossless under mismatched ones.

\section{Scope and limitations}
%======================================================================

ORL faces are cropped, frontal, aligned, and captured against a constant
background in one laboratory, with no clutter, occlusion, or age variation. No
detection or alignment stage was required. In deployed systems alignment error
is often the dominant failure source, so these accuracies do not estimate
unconstrained performance.

PCA is unsupervised and maximises total variance without reference to labels,
which is consistent with the leading components encoding illumination.
Fisherfaces \citep{belhumeur1997}, maximising between-class to within-class
scatter, typically does better under lighting variation. That comparison was
not implemented here, however, so the claim that PCA is suboptimal rests on the
observed component structure and the illumination experiment rather than on a
direct measurement.

The model is linear, while pose and expression move a face along a curved
manifold a linear subspace only approximates. The scale is small. Even across
ten splits the standard deviation at the operating point is about $0.014$, so
differences of $0.01$ or $0.02$ have no interpretation here. Accuracy also
falls as the gallery grows, so these figures would not transfer to thousands of
identities. The illumination model is synthetic, omitting cast shadows,
specularities, and saturation, all non-linear effects likely to degrade
performance further. The setting is closed-set: nearest-neighbour
classification always returns some identity, whereas a deployed system requires a
rejection threshold, a separate problem.

\section{Summary and conclusions}
%======================================================================

Recovering the eigenfaces from a $280\times280$ eigenproblem instead of a
$4096\times4096$ one ran 614 times faster on the machine used and matched a
reference implementation to machine precision. The leading
eigenfaces encode photometric conditions rather than identity, a consequence of
ranking directions by variance and supported by the illumination experiment.
Recognition accuracy saturates at a few tens of components while
reconstruction error keeps falling, so the conventional 95\% variance threshold
corresponds to roughly four times the components required for recognition. Retaining more
components does not necessarily generalise better, and whether it degrades
accuracy depends on the metric: plain Euclidean 1-NN plateaus, where a whitened
metric degrades as noise-dominated components are amplified.

Enrolment set size has the largest effect of the factors tested, moving accuracy from
$0.950$ with nine training images per subject to $0.547$ with one. Unseen
illumination is the most damaging perturbation, and the effective remedy is to
discard leading components, not to change the distance measure. Cosine
distance, often recommended for this purpose, gave no benefit against spatially
varying lighting.

All results come from \texttt{notebooks/eigenfaces\_project.ipynb}, which runs
top to bottom, writes every figure to \texttt{figures/} and every number to
\texttt{report/results.json}, and uses fixed seeds so the reported accuracies
reproduce exactly; the timings are wall-clock measurements from one machine.
