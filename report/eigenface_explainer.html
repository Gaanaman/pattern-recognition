Plate 01

Pixel space is nearly all emptiness

A 64×64 greyscale photograph is a list of 4,096 numbers, so it is a single point in 4,096-dimensional space. That framing sounds abstract until you ask what else lives in that space.

Press the button. Each image is a legitimate point in pixel space, drawn at random. None of them is a face, and none of them ever will be. Faces occupy a vanishingly thin sliver of the space they are stored in, and that redundancy is the whole opportunity.

Plate 02

Subtract what every face has in common

Average all 280 training photographs pixel by pixel and you get the mean face: blurred, generic, no one in particular. It holds what faces share — two eyes above a nose above a mouth, roughly aligned.

Subtract it from a photograph and what remains is that person's deviation from the average. Red is brighter than average, blue darker. Identity lives in the deviation, not in the shared structure, which is why every step from here works on centred images.

Plate 03

The directions that matter most

Principal component analysis asks: along which direction do these 280 faces vary the most? Then, which direction next, at right angles to the first? The answers are eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, and each one is itself a list of 4,096 numbers — so each one can be shown as an image. Hence eigenfaces.

An eigenface is not a person. It is a coordinated pattern of departure from the mean. Click any of them.

The catch that matters later

The first three eigenfaces carry no identity at all. They are lighting: overall contrast, overall brightness, and a top-to-bottom gradient. PCA ranks directions by variance, and changing the lamp moves thousands of pixels at once, so lighting wins first place. Plate 08 shows what that costs.

Plate 04

A face is a list of coefficients

Once you have the eigenfaces, describing a person no longer means storing 4,096 pixels. It means storing how much of each eigenface to add to the mean. Those amounts are the coefficients z, and they are the entire identity as far as this system is concerned.

Move the sliders. You are writing a face by hand, six numbers at a time. Sliders run ±3 standard deviations, so the extremes are faces this population could plausibly contain.

Plate 05

How few numbers before the person disappears?

This is the experiment the whole project turns on. Take a real photograph, keep only its first k coefficients, throw the rest away, and rebuild. At k = 1 you get the mean face with the lights adjusted. Somewhere on the way to 150 the person comes back.

Plate 06

More components does not mean better recognition

Now the machine's turn. Each face becomes its k coefficients, and a new photograph is identified by finding its nearest neighbour among the stored ones. Accuracy is measured over 10 independent train/test splits.

The two curves use the same eigenfaces and differ only in how distance is measured. Plain leaves each coefficient at its natural scale. Whitened divides each by its standard deviation, so every direction counts equally. Hover the chart.

Recognition accuracy against khover for values
Plain projection Whitened projection

The plain curve climbs and then flattens. It cannot fall: the eigenface basis is a rotation, so with every component kept, distance in eigenface space equals distance in pixel space exactly. Trailing components have tiny eigenvalues and barely register.

The whitened curve peaks at k = 20 and then collapses from 0.944 to 0.778. Dividing by a tiny standard deviation promotes a trailing direction to full weight — and those directions were estimated from 280 photographs in 4,096 dimensions, so they describe the quirks of this particular sample rather than anything about faces. That is overfitting, made visible.

The lesson

How many components to keep is not a property of the data alone. It is a property of the data and the classifier reading it. Change the distance measure and the right answer changes with it.

Plate 07

Looking right and being identifiable are different jobs

Here are both criteria on one pair of axes. Reconstruction error keeps falling all the way to k = 279. Recognition accuracy stops improving around k = 25.

Two criteria, two answerslower error · higher accuracy
Reconstruction error (left axis) Recognition accuracy (right axis) Thresholds

The gap between the two dashed lines is the finding. The textbook rule — keep enough components for 95% of the variance — asks for 103 components. Recognition is finished at 25. The extra 78 components buy about one accuracy point, which is smaller than the noise between splits.

The variance in that range is real; it is skin texture, sensor grain, small expression detail. It makes the picture better. It does not help tell one person from another.

Plate 08

Change the lighting and it falls apart

Now the promised cost of Plate 03. The test photographs get a left-to-right brightness ramp — a lamp that was not there when the system enrolled these people. The stored faces are untouched.

Euclidean nearest-neighbour falls from 0.937 to 0.142. The system is confusing "lit differently" with "different person", exactly as Plate 03 predicts.

Cosine distance, the usual recommendation, does nothing here: it discards the length of a vector, but a ramp that brightens one cheek and darkens the other rotates the vector instead of lengthening it. What does work is deleting the first three eigenfaces — the ones holding 46% of all the variance in the dataset. Recognition holds at 0.783. Throwing away the largest directions makes the system better.

Answer

How much can you throw away?

Of 4,096 dimensions, about 99% can go with no measurable loss of identity. Twenty-five coefficients match nearest-neighbour on the full raw pixel space, 0.926 against 0.934, a difference well inside the split-to-split noise.

Three things keep that honest. The ceiling comes from sample size, not from faces: 280 training photographs admit at most 279 components, so a larger dataset would answer differently. The number depends on which criterion you use, since reconstruction wants several times more. And it depends on conditions matching — Plate 08 is what happens when they do not.
